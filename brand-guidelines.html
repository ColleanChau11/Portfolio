Building a brand from a logo

Turning a company with only a logo into a full set of brand guidelines the whole team could use.

Data on the Spot · Brand guidelines · 2025 to 2026

My contributions: I wrote the audience, purpose, mission and vision. I also chose the typography, added the secondary colours, set the logo clear space rules, made the DOTSConnect logo versions and set the icon style. I worked with a marketing colleague and presented the result to the company president.

Overview

Data on the Spot (DOTS) makes voting and governance software for unions and associations. I was its Digital Design and Marketing Coordinator. When I started, the company had a logo and not much else. Over roughly three to four weeks, I helped turn that logo into brand guidelines the team could use for everything from LinkedIn posts to printed posters. I also met with the company president to check that the message and the look were right before we finalized anything.

The problem

The only brand asset was the logo: two blue figures and two grey ones spelling out DOTS, with small signal arcs above their heads. There was no chosen typeface, no secondary colours, no written voice and no rules for how to use any of it.

That caused several problems. There was no clear place to find the logo. People estimated the brand colours by eye, so the shades kept changing from one piece to the next. Anyone who needed assets had to contact several different people to get them. It showed up in the product too: the software used a purple that had been chosen at random, the DOTSConnect posts used a different font from everything else, and the DOTSConnect logo was too similar to the main DOTS logo.

Before the guidelines: the purple, the blues and the fonts all differ from piece to piece.

DOTS clients are mostly union and association leaders between 50 and 70, along with executive directors, governance professionals and event planners. They make important decisions for their members, but many of them don’t think of themselves as tech people. Some are moving off paper and manual systems for the first time. That shaped every choice: the brand couldn’t look like startup software. It had to feel steady and trustworthy, and it had to be easy to read.

What I did

  • Started with the logo. The two main colours are the logo’s own blue (#424A97) and grey (#BFC2C8), so the rest of the brand felt connected to the logo instead of added on later.
  • Wrote the foundations with my colleague. I wrote the audience, purpose, mission and vision pages. My colleague wrote the brand personality, and my boss created the slogan, “Confidence in every vote.” The personality is described in plain terms: confident but not flashy, clear and structured, calm under pressure, human and purpose-driven, professional but approachable. One line sums it up: if competitors sound like software companies, DOTS should sound like a trusted partner in the room.
  • Built the visual system. I added the secondary colours for text and backgrounds: charcoal, grey and a light cool grey. People were using a different font every time, so I chose two and gave each a job. Inter is for documents and the software, where small text needs to stay readable. Poppins is for headlines on social posts and printed pieces, where a friendlier look suits. Both use the same sizes: bold for headlines at 32 to 40 pt, semibold for subheaders at 18 to 24 pt, and regular for body text at 12 to 14 pt.
  • Set rules for the logo. I defined clear space around the logo so it stays readable on dark and light backgrounds and in small spaces. I also made DOTSConnect logo versions for the company’s connection product, using the same DOTS font. The product needed its own logo so people wouldn’t confuse it with the main DOTS brand, and we decided on a word logo.
  • Set the icon style. I chose a simple icon style (2px stroke, from the Lucide and Tabler libraries) to match the logo and the typography.

The president’s review

Once the draft was ready, I walked the president through it as a slideshow, one section at a time, to check that the message and the look matched how the company wanted to be seen. He liked the direction and asked for it to be tighter. I agreed: my first version was a bit too generic, and his feedback made two parts sharper.

The audience. He narrowed it and made it more specific. The final version names exactly who we serve: union leaders and executives, association administrators, event planners and convention organizers, and the delegates and members who take part in votes.

The mission. He tightened it. The final version says plainly that DOTS helps unions, associations and member-driven organizations run secure votes, hybrid meetings and large conventions, so leaders can focus on leadership and not logistics.

What it led to

The guidelines became the reference for anyone making materials at DOTS. I used them for all the guide book material and for our brand posts. Some of my colleagues used them for the software, and others used them to keep their slideshows on brand. They told me it was a lot easier to make things because all the colours and fonts were already provided.

Here are printed banners, social posts and the DOTSConnect Admin Guides, all made with the final guidelines.

What I’d do differently

I’d add two things. First, a one-page quick reference with the logo, the two main colours, the secondary colours and the fonts, so people wouldn’t have to open the whole guidelines document. Second, “do and don’t” examples, like the logo on the wrong background or stretched, so people could see mistakes before making them.

What I learned

Inconsistent branding usually isn’t people ignoring the brand. Nobody at DOTS was doing it wrong on purpose. There were no guidelines, so everyone made things the way they saw fit. Once people had clear colours, fonts and logo rules to work from, using them was easy.
